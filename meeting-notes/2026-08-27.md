- Title: A detector score is not evidence of learning
  Teaser: Pangram is an AI detection platform marketed to educators, developers, publishers, and trust-and-safety teams. A Pangram detector rated roughly one-third of a UC Berkeley math professor's op-ed as AI-generated or AI-assisted. When asked about this, the professor said AI helped edit an argument built through hundreds of hours of human work. Students took issue with the professors position indicating that students cannot use AI, when he used it to help create the op-ed.  
  
  On the surface this may seem inconsistent, but as I dig into it a bit more, I think it's quite different. Students in math are learning fundamental skills in the field, and learning those skills takes time and struggle.  By contrast, the professor is obviously an expert at writing, and does not need to develop those fundemantal skills.  I think this is exactly the use case that makes sense... something we can use our ideation for, but still ask for tips from the AI helper.  As you all know, I'm not a fan of AI detectors, but regardless of how convincing it is.. a detector score cannot tell us whether someone has the underlying expertise, followed the rules, or disclosed the help they used.

- Title: What should an AI data center owe Wisconsin Rapids?
  Analysis: A data center campus is proposed for the former paper mill property at 950 Fourth Avenue North in Wisconsin Rapids. It has not been approved. The city is reviewing the proposal, collecting public input, consulting experts, and considering what conditions or community protections would be needed if the project advances. The Planning Commission has also directed staff to draft a possible 12-month moratorium while the city studies public health, safety, infrastructure, and ratepayer equity. The developer points to construction work, long-term operations, tax-base growth, and new electrical infrastructure, but says specific job numbers will be refined later. Residents are asking about power, water, land use, noise, tax treatment, transparency, permanent jobs, and who pays when infrastructure assumptions change. Those are not anti-technology questions. They are the questions a community should answer before it makes a long-term infrastructure commitment.
  TheLesson: "Data centers create jobs" and "data centers consume resources" can both be true. I want the proposal made specific enough to evaluate: construction jobs, permanent jobs, training needs, power, water, backup generation, utility upgrades, tax treatment, community benefits, and who carries the risk. This is close enough to Mid-State that we should follow the facts, not the sales pitch or the fear.

Field Notes:
- Title: What a token actually is
  Content: If you use AI long enough, you will eventually hit a wall labeled "token limit," "context too long," or "usage remaining." Tokens are the unit those walls are measured in. They are not words, not characters, and not pages. They are the chunks the model can actually read and write.

    ## A token is a piece of text the model understands

    A language model does not see your prompt as a paragraph. It breaks the text into tokens first. A token might be a whole common word like "college." It might be a piece of a longer word. It might be punctuation, a space glued to a word, part of a URL, or a fragment of code. Rough English rule of thumb: about 100 tokens is roughly 75 words, or about 4 characters per token. Treat that as a sketch, not a ruler.

    ![A sentence broken into the tokens a model actually reads](token-split-inline.png)

    Why does this matter? Because every prompt you send, every file you attach, every earlier turn in the chat, and every sentence the model writes back all spend tokens from the same budget.

    ## The definition depends on the tokenizer

    Here is the part people usually skip. Different model families do not share one universal dictionary of tokens. Each provider uses a tokenizer, an algorithm that decides where to split text. OpenAI's GPT models commonly use byte-pair encoding through tools like tiktoken. Anthropic and Google use their own tokenizers. Same English sentence, different split, different count.

    That is why a paste that "fits" in one chat can overflow in another. The word "tokenization" itself may be one token in one system and two or three in another. Code, IDs, and non-English text often burn more tokens than plain prose because uncommon strings get chopped into smaller pieces. If two people argue about how long a document is in tokens, ask which model they counted against. Without that, the number is half-finished.

    ## How many tokens do ordinary tasks use?

    Approximate ranges for English workplace text, not guarantees:

    - A short email or a quick rewrite request: a few hundred tokens in, maybe a few hundred out.
    - A one-page memo or a dense paragraph of instructions: around 500 to 1,000 tokens.
    - A syllabus, rubric, or multi-page policy pasted into the chat: often several thousand tokens before the model writes a word.
    - A long PDF, meeting transcript, or full course packet: tens of thousands of tokens, sometimes enough to crowd out room for a useful answer.
    - A multi-turn conversation: earlier questions and answers stay in the window, so the meter keeps rising even if today's question is short.
    - Deep research or similar multi-step research modes: this is where usage jumps. The tool searches, opens sources, summarizes, retries, and drafts a long report. Each of those steps spends tokens, often far more than a normal chat answer to the same question.

    ![Relative token cost grows from a short email to deep research](token-task-sizes-inline.png)

    The expensive move is rarely the clever prompt. It is the giant attachment you did not need, the chat you never started fresh, or a deep research run when a focused question would have been enough.

    ## How many tokens do you actually have?

    Two different ceilings get mixed up.

    First is the context window: the maximum tokens one conversation can hold at once, input plus output. That is the model's working memory for this chat. When you fill it, older material gets truncated, ignored, or the request fails.

    Second is your account or institutional allowance: daily, monthly, or per-seat usage. You can have a huge context window and still run out of allowed usage for the month.

    Also separate input from output. Asking for a short outline costs less than asking for a full rewrite of a 20-page document. If you need the model to "remember" a whole course site, you are not just asking a question. You are renting a large share of its working memory.

    ## Other token ideas worth keeping straight

    - Context is cumulative. The model does not only count the latest message.
    - Summarize, select, or chunk before you paste everything.
    - Start a new chat when the topic changes. Old context is not free continuity. It is spent budget.
    - "Be concise" helps on the way out. Cleaning the source material helps on the way in.
    - Token count is also why agents get expensive. Each tool call and each retry adds another pass through the meter.

    I am putting together a short class on managing tokens for exactly this reason. The goal is not to make everyone a tokenizer expert. It is to help you see what you are spending, what is worth spending it on, and when a smaller, cleaner input will get you a better answer.

  Content: Use one scenario from the supplied deepfake-awareness video as a short staff drill. Ask what urgency, authority, or emotional trigger made the request persuasive. - Title: AI training reportedly destroyed the source material
  Content: A 404 Media investigation placed an AirTag in a rare book from a bulk order and followed it to an Amazon facility in Las Vegas. Workers told reporters that the operation cuts bindings from books so the pages can be scanned for AI training, destroying the physical copies in the process. Amazon confirmed that it purchases books through commercial channels to improve products and services, but the reporting does not establish the full scale of the operation or which models use the resulting data. Buying a copy may give a company ownership of that object. It does not make destroying rare source material the responsible way to build a dataset. Data provenance should include what was lost while the data was collected.
