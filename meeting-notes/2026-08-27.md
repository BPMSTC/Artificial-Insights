- Title: A detector score is not evidence
  Teaser: Pangram is an AI detection platform marketed to educators, developers, publishers, and trust-and-safety teams. Recently, a Pangram detector rated roughly one-third of a UC Berkeley math professor's op-ed as AI-generated. When asked about this, the professor said AI helped edit an argument built through hundreds of hours of human work. Students took issue with the professor's position indicating that students cannot use AI, when he used it to help create the op-ed.  
  
  On the surface this may seem inconsistent, but I think they're quite different situations. Students in math are learning fundamental skills in the field, and learning those skills takes time and struggle.  By contrast, the professor is obviously an expert at writing, and does not need to develop those fundamental skills.  I think this is exactly the use case that makes sense... something we can use our ideation for, but still ask for tips from the AI helper.  As you all know, I'm not a fan of AI detectors, but regardless of how convincing it is, a detector score cannot tell us whether someone has the underlying expertise, followed the rules, or disclosed the help they used.

- Title: What should an AI data center owe the city that hosts it?
  Analysis: As you are all aware, the United States is in the middle of an AI infrastructure boom and a simultaneous backlash. The backlash is becoming very loud in some areas of the country.  Hyperscale campuses are being proposed from former mill sites to farmland, while communities, utilities, and statehouses argue over power, water, rates, and who carries the risk. Data centers used about 4.4% of U.S. electricity in 2023 and are on a path toward 6.7–12% by 2028. 
  
  Large facilities can draw as much power as a city and as much cooling water as a small town; hundreds of planned sites sit in drought-stressed regions. That load is showing up in grid auctions, interconnection queues, and household bills. New York paused state environmental permits for large new facilities. Texas froze new grid connections pending audits of power, water, tax treatment, and ownership. Local governments in dozens of states have adopted moratoriums or bans while they study health, safety, and infrastructure. Developers point to construction work, tax-base growth, and new electrical infrastructure, but permanent job counts are often thin relative to capital invested, and specifics arrive late. 
  
  Residents across the country are asking the same questions: power, water, land use, noise, tax treatment, transparency, permanent jobs, and who pays when infrastructure assumptions change. These are often characterized as anti-technology questions, but I don't think so.  They are the questions a community should answer before it makes a long-term infrastructure commitment.
  TheLesson: "Data centers create jobs" and "data centers consume resources" can both be true. I think that many communities are on the same page and asking the right questions.  Take a look at the link showing the map of the data center bans. It's quite telling.

Field Notes:
- Title: What a token actually is
  Content: If you use AI long enough, you will eventually hit a wall labeled "token limit," "context too long," or "usage remaining." Tokens are the unit those walls are measured in. They are not words, not characters, and not pages. They are the chunks the model can actually read and write.

    ## A token is a piece of text the model understands

    A language model does not see your prompt as a paragraph. It breaks the text into tokens first. A token might be a whole common word like "college." It might be a piece of a longer word. It might be punctuation, a space glued to a word, part of a URL, or a fragment of code. Rough English rule of thumb: about 100 tokens is roughly 75 words, or about 4 characters per token.

    ![A sentence broken into the tokens a model actually reads](token-split-inline.png)

    Every prompt you send, every file you attach, every earlier turn in the chat, and every sentence the model writes back all spend tokens from the same budget.

    ## The definition depends on the tokenizer 
    
    Different model families do not share one universal dictionary of tokens. Each provider uses their own tokenizer.  Think of the tokenizer as an algorithm that decides where to split text. For example, OpenAI's GPT models use one called tiktoken. So the same English sentence could be tokenized in different ways depending on the model used.

    That is why doing the same work in one model may be more expensive than a second one, all due to the tokenizing.   

    ## How many tokens do ordinary tasks use?
    Approximate ranges for workplace text:

    - A short email or a quick rewrite request: a few hundred tokens in, maybe a few hundred out.

    - A one-page memo or a dense paragraph of instructions: around 500 to 1,000 tokens.

    - A syllabus, rubric, or multi-page policy pasted into the chat: often several thousand tokens before the model writes a single word.

    - A long PDF, meeting transcript, or full course packet: tens of thousands of tokens.

    - A multi-turn conversation: earlier questions and answers stay in the window, so the meter keeps rising. A long back-and-forth chat can reach a hundred thousand tokens even when today's question is short.

    - Deep research or similar multi-step research modes: this is where usage jumps. The tool searches, opens sources, summarizes, retries, and drafts a long report. Each of those steps spends tokens, often far more than a normal chat answer to the same question. A single deep research run can climb into the hundreds of thousands.

    - Eight full nonfiction books: around 1 million tokens.

    ![Relative token cost grows from a short email to deep research](token-task-sizes-inline.png)

    The expensive move is rarely the clever prompt. It is the giant attachment you did not need, the chat you never started fresh, or a deep research run when a focused question would have been enough.

    ## How the models talk about token limits... can be confusing
    Two different ceilings get mixed up.

    First is the context window: the maximum tokens one conversation can hold at once, input plus output. That is the model's working memory for this chat. When you fill it, older material gets truncated, ignored, or the request fails.

    Second is your account or institutional allowance: daily, monthly, or per-seat usage. You can have a huge context window and still run out of allowed usage for the month.

    Also separate input from output. Asking for a short outline costs less than asking for a full rewrite of a 20-page document. If you need the model to "remember" a whole course site, you are not just asking a question. You are renting a large share of its working memory.

    ## Other token ideas worth keeping straight

    - Context is cumulative. The model does not only count the latest message.
    - Summarize, select, or chunk before you paste everything.
    - Start a new chat **every time** the topic changes. Every time you go back and forth, the context is re-read and you are charged again.
    - "Be concise" as an instruction helps on the way out. Cleaning the source material helps on the way in.
    - Token count is also why agents get expensive. Each tool call and each retry adds another pass through the meter.

    I am putting together a short class on managing tokens for exactly this reason. The goal is not to make everyone a tokenizer expert. It is to help you get a feel for how to know what you are spending... from a token perspective.

- Title: Amazon bought rare books, cut them apart, and will not say what the scans feed
  Content: A 404 Media investigation hid an AirTag in a rare title inside a roughly 1,000-book Biblio order and followed it from a California airport through Milwaukee, a Kenosha warehouse, and Colorado to Amazon’s LAS8 site in Las Vegas. The investigation found that the workers there receive bulk printed shipments, cut the bindings so pages feed a scanner faster, scan barcodes and ISBNs first, and destroy the physical copy.
  
  When asked about this, Amazon’s only response was that it “purchases books through commercial channels to help develop and improve the products and services our customers use.” Ok, I'm going to go on a rant here because I'm a fan of books.  Buying a used book makes you the owner of the book, but that does not mean you should vacuum up scarce titles and choose the fastest method to carve up that valuable book. Non-destructive scanning exists, so there is no reason that can't be used. Remember that Amazon started as a bookseller, so if nothing else it's ironic they are destroying books now.

- Title: See what one task actually costs in tokens
  Intro: Pick one thing you do every week: answer a student email, summarize a meeting, or pull key points from a policy PDF. Most of us guess at the cost. This takes about five minutes and makes tokens feel real instead of abstract.
  ThePrompt: "I am going to describe a task I do regularly. Do not do the task yet. First, estimate roughly how many input tokens and output tokens it would use if I (1) pasted the full source document, (2) pasted only the relevant section, or (3) asked a focused question without attachments.
