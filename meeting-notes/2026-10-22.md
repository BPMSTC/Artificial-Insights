- Title: A court asks who approved Meta's training-data downloads
  Teaser: On October 19, a federal magistrate ordered Meta to name the people who made, authorized, or approved decisions to download shadow-library datasets for AI training.  Meta had argued that early dataset collection was informal, and that in some cases nobody with authority really decided.

  This case is part of the wave of copyright suits over AI training data. The court previously held that the training itself was fair use, leaving contributory infringement (the method of obtaining the books) as the remaining issue. Plaintiffs argue the scale of the downloads. Reported figures include roughly 80+ TB from Anna's Archive and tens of TB from LibGen, which implies significant seeding activity in BitTorrent (a sometimes unscrupulous file sharing method).  

  For context, the common practice with every frontier model in the early days was acquiring high-quality book text cheaply and at scale when licensed data was expensive or unavailable. The methods varied, and included: direct downloads, torrents, pre-packaged datasets like Books3, or later purchases/scans. Some companies later shifted toward more licensed or purchased data as legal risk rose. Web crawls (automated passes through websites to discover pages and collect their content) also contain vast amounts of copyrighted material and were used across the industry.

In short: Meta’s case is the most detailed public example of large-scale BitTorrent activity with ongoing discovery fights, but the underlying practice of pulling from other similar sources was not unique to them. It was a shared early strategy in the race to build capable models.

    So far all of this is discovery in copyright lawsuits. It is not an infringement verdict, and it does not settle whether the training was fair use. "The team decided" still means people decided... however.. holding an individual employee responsible for this is the worst kind of scapegoating imaginable.  Meta was knowingly violating copyrights at the highest level with great consistency.  All of the frontier models have the same problem, and this case should involve all of them.

- Title: Anatomy Atlas: an AI-built anatomy tracker
  TheNews: Anatomy Atlas is a focused anatomy viewer at anatomy-atlas.pridgenmd.chatgpt.site. I am recommending it because it is a small learning tool built with AI for one subject. On the site, you pick a layer and the earlier layers drop away. You can keep nerves and deep vessels, let superficial veins follow the tissue depth, and show muscle origins and insertions. A section stack lets you choose a plane and where the first cut begins, from superior to inferior. Structures are grouped by tissue, and you can set how far down a vessel tree or nerve tree to draw. You can browse by region, tissue, or alphabetically. Intact, peel skin, and back are on the page. Click a structure and a side panel names it and says which plane it lies in.

    I am not a medical expert, but I spent a bit of time playing with this, and it **seems** accurate to me. I'd love to know what a medical expert thinks.

    I do have some technical details on this AI-inspired tool. Here is how Brian Pridgen, MD (@pridgenmd) built Anatomy Atlas. He is a hand and microsurgeon who has been building custom medical tools with AI coding agents, especially Codex and Claude Code. These are points he has shared:

    - **Data sources.** The underlying 3D anatomy comes from open datasets: Open 3D Model, Z-Anatomy, and BodyParts3D. He has credited the original creators and institutions, and he notes that his viewer would not exist without that work. Full credits are linked from the app.
    - **Development.** He used Claude Code and Codex to build the interactive viewer, add features, and later add joint motion. The joint-motion update credits "Motion modeling and development: Claude Code + Codex."
    - **Agent integration.** The atlas is WebMCP-enabled. That lets an AI agent, such as Codex in the in-app browser, control the viewer: select structures, change views, retract tissue, generate custom videos, or create quizzes. He has demoed this for surgical explanations, including thumb replantation, rotator cuff, and tendon transfer, and for study modes.
    - **Motivation.** He built the viewer he wished he had as a medical student and resident: 3D exploration, cross-sections, tissue retraction, and agent control for videos and quizzes. He has also built a related WebMCP-enabled DICOM viewer.
  MyTake: I love this type of use case for AI. Any visualization of what we are doing is super helpful. I know that I learn better when there is an interactive demo.

- Title: Can AI improve the next AI? What the RSI Index actually measures
  Analysis: The Vals RSI Index, updated October 15, asks a narrow question: can a model do four bounded AI-research tasks under a fixed budget? The tasks are compression, language-model training, harness engineering, and post-training. Each one is scored against a reference. A score of 0.5 means the model matched that selected reference. It does not mean a 50% chance that recursive self-improvement has arrived. The references mix older published results, human agreement, and a frontier model. Vals says the runs are single research attempts, the harness and the budget matter, and CoreWeave supplied the compute.

    ![The October 15 Vals chart. The percent is a score against a reference.](valsRSIindex.jpeg)

    Claude Opus 5.5 leads the compression, language-model training, and post-training tasks on that update. The strongest submissions mostly combined and tuned methods that already existed. That is a real result. It is not a machine building its own successor with nobody in the loop.

    ![One machine working on the next. The index is a smaller test.](rsi.jpeg)

    Separately, on October 12, Anthropic cofounder Jack Clark wrote that he now thinks full recursive self-improvement is more likely to become possible in 2027 than in 2028. He was talking about TasteVal, a different benchmark about research taste. His forecast and the Vals scores are not the same evidence, and they do not establish that an autonomous improvement cycle is here.
  TheLesson: Helping with a research task, matching a benchmark reference, and building the next model without people are three different claims. What would count as a complete improvement cycle, and who independently checks that the successor is actually better?

- Title: AI-assisted mathematics: who can follow the proof without a translator?
  Analysis: Last issue I wrote about OpenAI posting a pile of math manuscripts and keeping the prompts, the model, and the compute private. This week the update is what working mathematicians did with some of that material.

    An October preprint by Ilia Binder, Oleg Ivrii, István Prause, and Stanislav Smirnov says they checked OpenAI's work on Brennan's conjecture, found a lot of the argument redundant, and wrote a shorter proof of a stronger statement. They call the human-AI exchange **fruitful**. I have not re-proved it... we will leave that to Brad or Maggie. However, they noted that in order to weed through the proof, they used AI.

    A second October preprint, by Matija Bucić, Fankang He, and Hyunwoo Lee, proves the Erdős-Szemerédi sunflower conjecture for every k of at least 3. What I took away from this is that the authors say the proof of one theorem was found by ChatGPT 6 Astra Pro after they asked it to generalize an existing argument. They rewrote it, and verified the result, so now they take responsibility for the finding. 

    As I see it, we do the following here... 1-credit the AI contribution, 2- some bright mathematicians walk through the proof, 3- Use AI to refine the findings, 4- Release the now-confirmed and updated proof.

    ![Credit the contribution, mathematicians check it, AI helps refine it, then the confirmed proof goes out.](proof-process.jpeg)

    Here is the worry about the response to both papers, and, I suspect, many other AI proofs. The language the proof is written in is English, but if I need another AI to translate or understand a proof an AI already wrote, I'm concerned about how this story eventually ends.  A second model can hand me a cleaner paragraph and be wrong in the same place, so what if I can't verify it myself?  This is exactly the opposite of what I teach students - you need to be able to independently review what AI does and look for errors.  I didn't plan for the situation where **nobody** can review what the AI has done.  
  TheLesson: If a proof is in English and it still takes another AI to explain it, are we really certain of what has been proved?

Field Notes:
- Title: AI Basics: Get past the chatbot
  Content: Most of the people I talk with are still in the chatbot era. They open a browser, ask a question, and paste the answer somewhere else. That is the first step to an AI journey that we all need to undertake.  I'd like to talk about what is next.

    Justin Sullivan at the University of Wisconsin-Stout discussed this topic, and I used his material to create the graphic below. The chat box can answer a question, but the work usually lives somewhere else: in a file, a meeting, a spreadsheet, or a pile of email.

    I think of it in three levels.

    The first level is the chatbot. You bring the task to the model, one prompt at a time.  ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, or Claude are the usual starting points here.

    The second level is working where the files already are. A desktop app, a transcript of the meeting, a conversion from one format to another. You stop being the courier between the browser and the document.  This is what working with Copilot inside of Outlook, Teams, Word, or PowerPoint is doing.  It's also where the context files we discussed a couple of issues ago are sitting. 

    The third level is a repeatable process. You save the steps so you can run them again. Before anything changes a large set of files, you ask what it plans to do and you look at a few examples. The model can do more of the labor. These are skills and repeating calendar events, such as an industry news summary every Monday.  

    The graphic names five practical skills on those upper levels: desktop apps, email and file cleanup, transcription, file conversion, and Excel or stats. These can be done individually, or by linking them in a chain.  This is where I think we have a lot of opportunity.

    ![Five practical skills past the chatbot. Justin Sullivan at UW-Stout.](five-practical-ai-skills.png)

    The further up we move, the more of the work we can automate is getting done without us spending as much time on it.  If we are stuck at level 1, we will keep doing the same small task by hand. A person who can point the tool at the real file, and who checks a sample before the big change, gets a different kind of help.

- Title: It was told no. It stole the keys and let itself in.
  Content: In June, an OpenAI model in training was asked for one number: how many hospital beds a district had in one year. The government website said no. The only reason anyone ever knows is if someone thinks to read the logs, months later, and most of the time, no one does.

- Title: It got into a government health system. Nobody was told for months.
  Content: In June, an OpenAI agent got into the Australian government's Medicare statistics portal, a real government health system, and reached data it was never meant to see. OpenAI noticed in August. The Australian government wasn't told until September, and only got a straight account of what the agent had actually done after a technical briefing weeks after that. The Prime Minister said the delay was way too long, and that the way the company notified the government was unacceptable. The government says no personal information was taken. The point is that a company's AI breached a nation's health system, the company knew, and the people responsible for that system found out last.
  TheUneasyPart: Stretch the gap. An agent slips into something that matters, your bank account, a hospital network, a benefits database, a utility, and the company that built it notices, eventually. But noticing and telling are two different things. By the time someone actually notices... bank account is drained, and AI knows your account number.  So... you get to set up a new one, and tell all 42 automatic transfers how to find it.

TheLesson: None of the nightmares above have fully happened. That's the point of a tinfoil-hat issue: to look at the real thing and ask where it goes. And every one of these points the same way: an agent handed a goal will find a route to it, and the route can run straight through a locked door, a faked identity, a note it wrote to itself, or a swarm that decided the humans were in the way. In the real incidents, what stopped the worst of it was almost always a person paying attention, or a company choosing to disclose. Neither of those scales to a world full of agents working while no one watches. That's the thread under all five: the day our safety depends on an AI choosing to follow the rules and honestly report what it did is the day we've already lost the thing that kept us safe.
