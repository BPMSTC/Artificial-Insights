Title: Stitch by Google

TheNews: Stitch is Google’s new interface-generation tool that lets you describe an app or screen in plain language and get a design back. It is aimed at quickly turning rough ideas into UI concepts without making people hand-place every sad little button themselves. Google is clearly leaning into “describe what you want and let the model decide how to build it” as a mainstream product pattern.

MyTake: This is one of the more useful tools I’ve seen lately for both educators and everyday office work. For instructors, it can help mock up a student app idea, dashboard concept, or classroom tool without opening a full design suite. For staff and productivity work, it is a fast way to prototype internal forms, portals, or workflow ideas before anybody burns a week building the wrong thing. It is practical, visual, and fast, which is more than can be said for most enterprise brainstorming meetings.

The Breakdown

Title: The White House Wants a National AI Rulebook. Some of It Makes Sense. Some of It Is Fantasy.

Analysis:
The biggest story in this issue is the Trump administration’s new national AI legislative framework. The broad goal is obvious: stop states from creating a patchwork of AI laws, push harder on infrastructure and adoption, and keep regulation light enough that the U.S. does not trip over its own shoelaces while China sprints ahead. On paper, some of this is reasonable. A single national approach is cleaner than fifty competing ones. Support for small business adoption, workforce training, anti-fraud enforcement, and protections against unauthorized digital replicas all belong in the conversation.

But then the document swerves into the usual AI-policy fever dream where every hard question gets flattened into “states are slowing innovation.” The framework argues for preempting state laws that impose “undue burdens,” limiting state authority over AI development itself, and blocking states from penalizing developers for unlawful conduct by third parties using their models. That is a huge shift of power upward, and it is dressed up as efficiency. Efficiency is the favorite perfume of people trying to remove brakes.

The intellectual property section is where things get especially messy. The framework says Congress should avoid prejudging ongoing litigation over whether training on copyrighted material is fair use, while also floating licensing or collective-rights approaches that could let compensation happen without immediately rewriting copyright law. That is more cautious than some hot takes online made it sound, but it still reflects the same underlying pressure: the industry wants legal certainty now, even though the courts have not finished doing their job.

What makes this more important is the timing. In the same month that Washington is pushing a national framework, the Senate has also approved ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot for use with official Senate data. That is the real signal. AI policy is no longer just about future risks. It is about normalizing AI inside the everyday machinery of government right now. Rules are being written at the same time the tools are being operationalized. That combination matters a lot more than another performative hearing with five senators pretending they discovered machine learning last Tuesday.

TheLesson:
For colleges and workplaces, the lesson is pretty simple: build your own governance before somebody else hands you a generic one. If national policy is heading toward broad adoption with limited friction, then local institutions need clearer rules on data use, consent, intellectual property, classroom discretion, and what kinds of AI use are actually worth doing. The winning move is not “ban everything” or “embrace everything.” It is deciding where AI helps, where it distorts, and where your people still need the right to say no.

Ed Pulse

Item 1
Title: Writing Faculty Push Back on Blanket AI Expectations
Content:
The CCCC resolution on refusing generative AI in the writing classroom is one of the clearest discipline-level responses to institutional AI overreach I’ve seen so far. It frames refusal in terms of academic freedom, student agency, language diversity, consent, and opposition to punitive surveillance. Even if you do not teach writing, the message is bigger than one department: AI policy cannot just be a one-way mandate from administration or vendors. Educators need room to decide where AI belongs, where it does not, and what students should be allowed to opt out of.

Item 2
Title: AI Retention Systems Are Getting More Aggressive
Content:
Another higher-ed trend worth watching is the growing use of AI to flag students at risk of dropping out. Institutions are using predictive systems to score retention risk, identify weak engagement patterns, and route students toward human intervention earlier. There is real promise here if the result is faster support and better advising. There is also the usual institutional temptation to treat dashboards as wisdom. A campus can absolutely use AI to find students who need help. It can also use AI to automate distance and call it care. The human follow-through is still the whole ballgame.

Try This

Title: Audit Your AI Rules Before Somebody Else Writes Them for You

Intro:
This week’s big theme is governance. AI is becoming normal inside government, classrooms, and productivity platforms at the same time that policy is still being improvised. So here is a ten-minute exercise to see whether your own rules are coherent or just vibes wearing a lanyard.

ThePrompt:
List three recurring places where AI shows up in your environment right now. For each one, answer four questions:

What data is being touched?
Who is allowed to use AI there?
What kind of human review is required before the output is used?
Do people have a clear right to opt out?

Then circle the one area where you realized the policy is either unclear, contradictory, or completely imaginary.
