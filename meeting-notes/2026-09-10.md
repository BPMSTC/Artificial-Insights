The Breakdown:
- Title: When AI opens the math proof factory, proofs get cheap while understanding stays expensive.
  Analysis: OpenAI says an internal model, one it has not released to the public, resolved the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem. The company describes it as significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra, released days earlier. The Clay Mathematics Institute picked that problem in 2000 as one of seven Millennium Prize Problems. Each carries a million-dollar prize, and six of the seven were still open. Starting from a smooth fluid at rest, under a smooth applied force, the proof drives it to a singularity in finite time. That establishes statements C and D in Clay's official formulation. The equations break down. The agents got there about 88 hours after launch, with Lean formalization and verification adding another 17 hours through GPT-6 Astra.

    The Navier–Stokes run burned roughly 130 billion output tokens, 2.7 million messages passing among the agents, about 10,000 of them running concurrently. Only a handful of frontier labs can spend like that. Cheap for OpenAI is not cheap for a college or a single mathematician. A Lean formalization is strong evidence that a stated theorem follows from stated assumptions. It does not tell you whether the right statement got formalized, or whether the field will accept what was proved. Credit is already contested. OpenAI started on September 1 after hearing a rumor about Levent Alpöge at Anthropic and Tristan Buckmaster at NYU. Those two had resolved the forced Euler problem instead, a separate result whose priority OpenAI has publicly recognized. OpenAI also says it will not claim the prize. Generation got faster and cheaper, while checking, interpretation, and teaching kept their old timelines and stayed with people.
  TheLesson: I'm careful when repeating "AI solved mathematics" without giving it a more careful check.  I'll make sure to ask what was claimed, what was formally checked, and who outside OpenAI has reviewed it. Right now the answer to that last question is nobody. A machine-checked proof is a serious artifact and deserves close reading, but I'm still going to require actual people to verify this before I'm totally convinced.

Field Notes:
- Title: AI Basics: What is a context window?
  Content: Last newsletter we talked about what a token is...one small piece of text that a model reads. A context window is the total amount of tokens the model can keep in view while it works on one request. Think of it as the size of the desk, not the size of the library. You can own a library full of documents, but the model can only spread so many pages across the desk at once.

    ## A bigger desk is not the same as understanding

    A larger context window helps when you need to work across a long policy, a course shell, a codebase, or a pile of meeting notes. Unfortunately, it does not mean the model understood every page, remembered it perfectly, or used the right part. The practical move is still to give the model a clear task, point it to the relevant material, and check the result against the source.

    ## Five ways to use the window well

    **Only look up what you need.** Don't hand the AI a whole 50-page manual when it only needs one paragraph. Search the manual first, grab just the relevant part, and give the AI only that. It's like using the library's catalog to pull one book instead of dumping the entire shelf onto your desk. Too much irrelevant info makes the AI's attention get "watered down" and start missing key facts.

    **Put the important stuff first or last.** AI models have a funny habit: they remember the beginning and the end of what you give them really well, but things in the middle tend to get forgotten. It's like how you remember the first and last items on a shopping list but lose the ones in the middle. So put your main instructions or your actual question at the very top or very bottom of your prompt, never buried in the middle.

    **Cut documents into sensible-sized pieces.** When you're storing documents for the AI to search, break them into small sections of about 150 to 450 words, with a little bit of overlap between pieces so nothing falls in the cracks. Pieces that are too tiny lose the surrounding meaning. Pieces that are huge just create clutter.

    ![Tokens fill the context window on the way in, then the model generates one token at a time on the way out](LLM_Context_Window.png)

    **Keep the conversation and the knowledge separate.** For a chatbot, don't try to remember every word ever said. Summarize older parts of the chat and keep only the recent messages in full. Save long-term facts and background info in a separate database, and pull out only the bits you need at the moment. Think of it as keeping a diary summary instead of a word-for-word transcript of your whole life.

    **Use clear labels and dividers.** Separate your instructions from your information using obvious markers like headings or tags, for example putting facts between lines like "=== FACTS ===". These act like signposts so the AI knows "this part is my instruction" versus "this part is background info," and can process each piece in order.

Try This:
- Title: Split the documentation from the chat on one AI-assisted task
  Intro: Remember, that the context window is more like a whiteboard than a filing cabinet.

    Pick one task in your course, program, or office that requires reference material. Run it twice. First attempt: paste the entire reference document into the chat along with your questions, and keep adding to the same chat as the task evolves. Second attempt: store the documentation somewhere separate, such as a file you upload, a pinned project doc, or a notebook, and use short, focused chats that quote or reference only the section you need. Start a new chat when the task changes. Make a note about where each version drifted, hallucinated, forgot earlier instructions, or got slower and more expensive.
  ThePrompt: "Here is my [reference document], stored in [location: attached file, project doc, or notebook]. Answer only from the section I reference. If the answer is not in that section, say 'not in the referenced material' instead of guessing. When I move to [next task or question], remind me to start a fresh chat, and carry over only the conclusions I explicitly give you, not the whole conversation."
  WhyItWorks: A context window is finite, and attention degrades as it fills. When everything lives in one ever-growing chat, the model "pays" to re-read your documentation on every turn, older instructions get diluted, and you can't tell what it actually used. Separating stable reference material from volatile conversation keeps the model anchored to a verifiable source, makes failures visible because you can point at the doc instead of arguing with the chat, and gives you a clean audit trail of what the AI was shown versus what it invented.
