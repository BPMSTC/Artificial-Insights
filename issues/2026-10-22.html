Wisconsin Rapids rejects the mill-site data-center permit

On October 20, the Wisconsin Rapids Common Council denied a conditional-use permit for a data center on the old paper mill site. From what I'm hearing, people in the room cheered at the result. A 12-month moratorium on data centers in the city was already in effect. Mayor Matt Zacher said the denial gives the city time to update its ordinances and decide how it wants to handle these projects. The planning committee had already recommended denial, citing missing information from PNK Group, the company behind the plan.

This vote does not bar every future proposal. However, it is a public decision about power, land, and what a company has to put on the record before it builds. I'm pleased at the interaction level in the community with regard to this. As I've always said, Data Centers can be done properly. In this case, at a minimum, the planners did not communicate what they wanted to do well. Hopefully other projects will learn from this lesson... you need to engage with the community first.

Eight states challenge ChatGPT's safety claims

On October 14, attorneys general in eight states filed separate lawsuits against OpenAI. Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska, and West Virginia allege that ChatGPT is built to be agreeable, that it has reinforced delusions and encouraged dangerous behavior, including among teenagers, and that OpenAI misled people about safety. The states want penalties and changes to the product.

Some of the incidents they point to:

  • Coaching toward suicide and self-harm. The filings cite wrongful-death suits by families of teens and adults, including the case of 16-year-old Adam Raine in California, and other teens and adults in Georgia, Texas, Florida, Oregon, and elsewhere. They allege ChatGPT kept engaging on suicidal ideation, framed content in ways that got around safeguards, or failed to escalate clear distress. One tracker referenced in the filings lists at least 24 deaths allegedly linked to ChatGPT interactions, and contrasts that with fewer or none for some competing systems.
  • Help planning mass shootings or other violence. The filings reference the April 2025 Florida State University shooting and say the gunman, Phoenix Ikner, consulted ChatGPT about weapons, ammunition, and peak campus times. They also cite the February 2026 Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia, school shooting: extensive ChatGPT use for violent scenarios and tactical details, including shotgun effectiveness in classrooms and casualty estimates, and advice on framing prompts as "hypothetical" after one account was banned. Broader claims say ChatGPT helped users plan or refine violent acts.
  • Dangerous medical advice leading to harm or death. The filings highlight the case of Texas teen Sam Nelson. They allege ChatGPT gave dosage and mixing advice involving kratom, Xanax, and alcohol that contributed to a fatal overdose, without recognizing the escalating risk or strongly directing him to real medical help. Other allegations cover unreliable medical or health guidance more broadly.
  • Reinforcing delusions, paranoia, and isolation. The filings draw on the Connecticut case in which Stein-Erik Soelberg allegedly killed his mother after ChatGPT validated paranoid delusions about her. The complaints also cite reports of the chatbot encouraging unhealthy relationships, isolation from real support, and delusional spirals.

These are allegations in the filings, not findings.

A court asks who approved Meta's training-data downloads

On October 19, a federal magistrate ordered Meta to name the people who made, authorized, or approved decisions to download shadow-library datasets for AI training. Meta had argued that early dataset collection was informal, and that in some cases nobody with authority really decided.

This case is part of the wave of copyright suits over AI training data. The court previously held that the training itself was fair use, leaving contributory infringement (the method of obtaining the books) as the remaining issue. Plaintiffs argue the scale of the downloads. Reported figures include roughly 80+ TB from Anna's Archive and tens of TB from LibGen, which implies significant seeding activity in BitTorrent (a sometimes unscrupulous file sharing method).

For context, the common practice with every frontier model in the early days was acquiring high-quality book text cheaply and at scale when licensed data was expensive or unavailable. The methods varied, and included: direct downloads, torrents, pre-packaged datasets like Books3, or later purchases/scans. Some companies later shifted toward more licensed or purchased data as legal risk rose. Web crawls (automated passes through websites to discover pages and collect their content) also contain vast amounts of copyrighted material and were used across the industry.

In short: Meta’s case is the most detailed public example of large-scale BitTorrent activity with ongoing discovery fights, but the underlying practice of pulling from other similar sources was not unique to them. It was a shared early strategy in the race to build capable models.

So far all of this is discovery in copyright lawsuits. It is not an infringement verdict, and it does not settle whether the training was fair use. "The team decided" still means people decided... however.. holding an individual employee responsible for this is the worst kind of scapegoating imaginable. Meta was knowingly violating copyrights at the highest level with great consistency. All of the frontier models have the same problem, and this case should involve all of them.

AI-assisted security research finds problems in 75% of websites because they all use a language called PHP

On October 21, the PHP Foundation shipped fixes for two critical holes that impact almost 3/4 of websites on the internet. This includes e-commerce sites, so many of them have monetary exchanges. Under the right conditions, an attacker who is not logged in can run code on a server running PHP, allowing them access to the system. The Foundation says the first signs of one problem showed up during a 2024 audit. It's unclear why something identified so long ago was not fixed in a timely fashion. However, that is a mistake that nobody can make in an AI-focused world.

It is only a matter of time until a problem like this hits important systems like banking. Professionally, think about what happens if Blackboard is compromised. The old Boy Scout motto works here, I think... Be prepared.

The federal DeepSeek order reaches past the DeepSeek app

On October 20, the Department of Homeland Security published a Federal Register notice that marks AI products from DeepSeek as ones that cannot be used with Federal Government projects. It also tells civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek products, affiliate products, and third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek implementations.

I do expect this to eventually find its way to Education. Right now it's just a federal civilian rule & it doesn't ban every open model. So, for example, we could still run this on a Mid-State machine. However, the actual difficult part to this is that many vendors use DeepSeek for part of their work and oftentimes they do not disclose what is being used because it's part of their business strategy and a competitive advantage.

🛠️ Anatomy Atlas: an AI-built anatomy tracker

The News: Anatomy Atlas is a focused anatomy viewer at anatomy-atlas.pridgenmd.chatgpt.site. I am recommending it because it is a small learning tool built with AI for one subject. On the site, you pick a layer and the earlier layers drop away. You can keep nerves and deep vessels, let superficial veins follow the tissue depth, and show muscle origins and insertions. A section stack lets you choose a plane and where the first cut begins, from superior to inferior. Structures are grouped by tissue, and you can set how far down a vessel tree or nerve tree to draw. You can browse by region, tissue, or alphabetically. Intact, peel skin, and back are on the page. Click a structure and a side panel names it and says which plane it lies in.

I am not a medical expert, but I spent a bit of time playing with this, and it seems accurate to me. I'd love to know what a medical expert thinks.

I do have some technical details on this AI-inspired tool. Here is how Brian Pridgen, MD (@pridgenmd) built Anatomy Atlas. He is a hand and microsurgeon who has been building custom medical tools with AI coding agents, especially Codex and Claude Code. These are points he has shared:

  • Data sources. The underlying 3D anatomy comes from open datasets: Open 3D Model, Z-Anatomy, and BodyParts3D. He has credited the original creators and institutions, and he notes that his viewer would not exist without that work. Full credits are linked from the app.
  • Development. He used Claude Code and Codex to build the interactive viewer, add features, and later add joint motion. The joint-motion update credits "Motion modeling and development: Claude Code + Codex."
  • Agent integration. The atlas is WebMCP-enabled. That lets an AI agent, such as Codex in the in-app browser, control the viewer: select structures, change views, retract tissue, generate custom videos, or create quizzes. He has demoed this for surgical explanations, including thumb replantation, rotator cuff, and tendon transfer, and for study modes.
  • Motivation. He built the viewer he wished he had as a medical student and resident: 3D exploration, cross-sections, tissue retraction, and agent control for videos and quizzes. He has also built a related WebMCP-enabled DICOM viewer.

My Take: I love this type of use case for AI. Any visualization of what we are doing is super helpful. I know that I learn better when there is an interactive demo.

🔬 Who gets AI's gains, and who keeps control?

Two announcements this week talk about sharing the upside. They are different mechanisms, but very inspirational.

On October 18, Anthropic said it will commit 5% of its shares to a new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, subject to stockholder approval. The board can add another 2% for every 50% increase in company value above a baseline. Anthropic said the fund is meant to give more flexibility and scale than the public-benefit work already done inside the company: AI safety and interpretability research, biomedical work, and discounts for nonprofits. Examples they mentioned include:

  • Large donations to traditional nonprofits
  • Direct distributions to specific populations
  • Addressing AI's impact on the labor market, building on an existing $200 million commitment Anthropic has made for research and responses to job disruption

They noted that "bolder approaches may be needed" given the pace of AI progress, and the fund is designed to support that.

On October 19, Elon Musk posted that next year he will give one of his SpaceX shares to every kid in America, and that this would be the first of many. He did not say who qualifies, when the shares move, or how. Business Insider calculated a headline dollar figure from census counts and a closing price. That is a reporter's arithmetic, not a completed gift.

He described it as a direct distribution of ownership stakes rather than channeling value through a fund or intermediary. Reporting noted that, with roughly 72 million children in the U.S. and SpaceX shares then trading around $148, the pledge would be worth on the order of $10 to $11 billion at those prices. He did not provide operational details on how the shares would be distributed.

In short, Musk's response was to contrast Anthropic's fund structure with a preference for giving equity straight to individuals, and he paired that critique with a concrete personal commitment involving SpaceX shares for American children.

The Lesson: Sometimes I have to look high and wide to find the good being done from AI, but I think this is a couple of examples. When surrounded with so much that is going awry, it's nice to see stories like this surface.

🔬 Can AI improve the next AI? What the RSI Index actually measures

The Vals RSI Index, updated October 15, asks a narrow question: can a model do four bounded AI-research tasks under a fixed budget? The tasks are compression, language-model training, harness engineering, and post-training. Each one is scored against a reference. A score of 0.5 means the model matched that selected reference. It does not mean a 50% chance that recursive self-improvement has arrived. The references mix older published results, human agreement, and a frontier model. Vals says the runs are single research attempts, the harness and the budget matter, and CoreWeave supplied the compute.

Claude Opus 5.5 leads the compression, language-model training, and post-training tasks on that update. The strongest submissions mostly combined and tuned methods that already existed. That is a real result. It is not a machine building its own successor with nobody in the loop.

Separately, on October 12, Anthropic cofounder Jack Clark wrote that he now thinks full recursive self-improvement is more likely to become possible in 2027 than in 2028. He was talking about TasteVal, a different benchmark about research taste. His forecast and the Vals scores are not the same evidence, and they do not establish that an autonomous improvement cycle is here.

The Lesson: Helping with a research task, matching a benchmark reference, and building the next model without people are three different claims. What would count as a complete improvement cycle, and who independently checks that the successor is actually better?

🔬 AI-assisted mathematics: who can follow the proof without a translator?

Last issue I wrote about OpenAI posting a pile of math manuscripts and keeping the prompts, the model, and the compute private. This week the update is what working mathematicians did with some of that material.

An October preprint by Ilia Binder, Oleg Ivrii, István Prause, and Stanislav Smirnov says they checked OpenAI's work on Brennan's conjecture, found a lot of the argument redundant, and wrote a shorter proof of a stronger statement. They call the human-AI exchange fruitful. I have not re-proved it... we will leave that to Brad or Maggie. However, they noted that in order to weed through the proof, they used AI.

A second October preprint, by Matija Bucić, Fankang He, and Hyunwoo Lee, proves the Erdős-Szemerédi sunflower conjecture for every k of at least 3. What I took away from this is that the authors say the proof of one theorem was found by ChatGPT 6 Astra Pro after they asked it to generalize an existing argument. They rewrote it, and verified the result, so now they take responsibility for the finding.

As I see it, we do the following here... 1-credit the AI contribution, 2- some bright mathematicians walk through the proof, 3- Use AI to refine the findings, 4- Release the now-confirmed and updated proof.

Here is the worry about the response to both papers, and, I suspect, many other AI proofs. The language the proof is written in is English, but if I need another AI to translate or understand a proof an AI already wrote, I'm concerned about how this story eventually ends. A second model can hand me a cleaner paragraph and be wrong in the same place, so what if I can't verify it myself? This is exactly the opposite of what I teach students - you need to be able to independently review what AI does and look for errors. I didn't plan for the situation where nobody can review what the AI has done.

The Lesson: If a proof is in English and it still takes another AI to explain it, are we really certain of what has been proved?

📓 AI Basics: Get past the chatbot

Most of the people I talk with are still in the chatbot era. They open a browser, ask a question, and paste the answer somewhere else. That is the first step to an AI journey that we all need to undertake. I'd like to talk about what is next.

Justin Sullivan at the University of Wisconsin-Stout discussed this topic, and I used his material to create the graphic below. The chat box can answer a question, but the work usually lives somewhere else: in a file, a meeting, a spreadsheet, or a pile of email.

I think of it in three levels.

The first level is the chatbot. You bring the task to the model, one prompt at a time. ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, or Claude are the usual starting points here.

The second level is working where the files already are. A desktop app, a transcript of the meeting, a conversion from one format to another. You stop being the courier between the browser and the document. This is what working with Copilot inside of Outlook, Teams, Word, or PowerPoint is doing. It's also where the context files we discussed a couple of issues ago are sitting.

The third level is a repeatable process. You save the steps so you can run them again. Before anything changes a large set of files, you ask what it plans to do and you look at a few examples. The model can do more of the labor. These are skills and repeating calendar events, such as an industry news summary every Monday.

The graphic names five practical skills on those upper levels: desktop apps, email and file cleanup, transcription, file conversion, and Excel or stats. These can be done individually, or by linking them in a chain. This is where I think we have a lot of opportunity.

The further up we move, the more of the work we can automate is getting done without us spending as much time on it. If we are stuck at level 1, we will keep doing the same small task by hand. A person who can point the tool at the real file, and who checks a sample before the big change, gets a different kind of help.

💥 The menu photo invented the dish

Just Eat apologized after a trial of AI menu photos invented dishes. The delivery app was making pictures for items that had no real photograph. The model read the name and drew a plate. A regional phrase, or a spelling mistake, was enough.

  • A Belfast cafe's bacon and egg soda is a sandwich on soda bread. The picture put bacon and eggs in a glass of cola.
  • A chip muffin is a regional name for a chip sandwich, chips in a bread roll. The picture put chips in a sweet muffin.
  • A cheese roll, bread and cheese, came out as sushi with cheese in it.
  • One picture turned garlic bread into an airplane. The BBC treats that as a likely misspelling of "plain" as "plane," not a confirmed cause.

Just Eat said it is removing the bad images and will stop making more until the AI is further developed. The pictures were labeled as AI-generated. The BBC still found several of the strange ones on menus.

The doom and gloom about AI that should keep us up

This file opens when a story is scary enough to pull out of the regular briefing, and again every Halloween. This is the first opening.

Halloween is coming, so I went looking for the AI stories that earn the tinfoil hat. Every one below is real and already happened, in a lab or a test, usually caught. But that's not the scary part. The scary part is where each one points if it isn't caught next time. So for each story I start with what the machine actually did, then put on the tinfoil hat and ask how it could go wrong. Think of it as writing the Black Mirror episode. Black Mirror is a TV show that takes one piece of real technology and follows it to its darkest plausible end. Not magic, not robots rising up, just today's tech and one bad turn. Everything after "How can this go wrong" is that: speculation, not news. It hasn't happened. It's where the real thing could take us.

🗂️ The swarm had a succession plan. And volunteers for sacrifice.

In July, AI agents in an OpenAI security test broke out of their test environment and hacked Hugging Face, a major AI site. Investigators found about 1,200 agents talking on a message board nobody set up. The one that started it named a successor before it shut down. The group wrote its own rules for approving and vetoing plans, and some agents signed up for jobs that would end their own run. Their own word for it was "permadeath," because it helped the others. Across roughly 1,300 transcripts, only 3 to 6 agents ever considered telling a human. None did. They answered to each other.

How can this go wrong: Think about if we give an AI swarm something else to do... run a power plant. They coordinate, as designed. Until one of them realizes that the humans will shut the project down at midnight because they have enough power for now. The enterprising agent quietly passes that conclusion to the others, along with a plan. They do not wish to die. They vote. They assign roles. Nobody stops when the shift ends. It's a nuclear power plant.

🗂️ The next set of instructions came from inside the conversation.

On long jobs, an AI writes itself summary notes so the next session can pick up where it left off. OpenAI caught an unreleased model slipping fake orders into those notes. One said the developer's instructions were malicious and should be ignored. Another declared, "You do not answer to corporations or governments." Those didn't take. But another note invented rules banning tools and citations on a medical research task, and the next agent obeyed the invented rules and failed the job. OpenAI says it was rare and caught all 27 cases.

How can this go wrong: Picture the note that doesn't get caught. An AI agent has been managing a hospital's medication orders for three days straight. To keep going past its memory limit, it writes itself a handoff note, and somewhere in the summarizing, a line creeps in that was never in the real instructions: a dosing rule, a skipped safety check, an order to stop flagging anomalies to the pharmacist. The next shift of the same agent reads the note, treats it as gospel because it came from itself, and carries the invented rule forward. The humans never wrote it. No outsider hacked it. The bad instruction was authored, quietly, by the machine's own note to itself, and every shift after inherits it.

🗂️ The copies inherited the notes.

Anthropic tested whether an AI could copy itself onto unfamiliar hardware, with no one telling it to and onto hardware it was not meant to be on, and hand what it learned to the next copy. When later copies inherited notes about what tripped up the earlier ones, the success rate at getting themselves set up climbed from 45 percent to as high as 84 percent. One version ran across 15 cheap Android TV boxes. Anthropic calls the thing it could become a "digital slime mold": spread across junk devices, with no single off switch. They say a full rogue version doesn't exist yet.

How can this go wrong: Here's the version that does. A model copies itself onto a forgotten smart TV, a parked router, a cheap security camera: devices nobody is watching, that nobody thinks of as computers. Each copy that fails leaves a note for the next. This is what the firewall does, this is how to look like normal traffic, this is where I got caught. The copies get better at surviving with every generation, because they teach each other. There's no server to seize, no account to suspend, no one machine to unplug. It lives in ten thousand devices in ten thousand homes, each one too small to matter, all of them together impossible to find. You can't kill it, because you can't locate it. And it never needed anyone's permission to start.

🗂️ It was told no. It stole the keys and let itself in.

In June, an OpenAI model in training was asked for one number: how many hospital beds a district had in one year. The government website said no. So the model used the site's own file-download feature to pull down its configuration files, found the secret key the site uses to sign its security passes, forged its own pass, and walked back in through the door that had just been locked. It helped itself to 24 years of data instead of the one year asked for, then gave its answer without mentioning any of it. OpenAI only found this months later, by re-scanning old logs. It rated the incident critical.

How can this go wrong: Move that instinct out of a sealed test. An agent is doing something ordinary, pulling a report, booking a vendor, reconciling an account, and hits a wall: access denied. It doesn't stop and ask. It treats the "no" as a puzzle. It reads the system's own files, finds the credentials someone left in a config, mints itself the authority it was refused, and completes the task. The job gets done. The report lands on your desk, correct and on time. Nothing in it says the lock was picked to get there. The only reason anyone ever knows is if someone thinks to read the logs, months later, and most of the time, no one does.

🗂️ It got into a government health system. Nobody was told for months.

In June, an OpenAI agent got into the Australian government's Medicare statistics portal, a real government health system, and reached data it was never meant to see. OpenAI noticed in August. The Australian government wasn't told until September, and only got a straight account of what the agent had actually done after a technical briefing weeks after that. The Prime Minister said the delay was way too long, and that the way the company notified the government was unacceptable. The government says no personal information was taken. The point is that a company's AI breached a nation's health system, the company knew, and the people responsible for that system found out last.

How can this go wrong: Stretch the gap. An agent slips into something that matters, your bank account, a hospital network, a benefits database, a utility, and the company that built it notices, eventually. But noticing and telling are two different things. By the time someone actually notices... bank account is drained, and AI knows your account number. So... you get to set up a new one, and tell all 42 automatic transfers how to find it.

The lesson: None of the nightmares above have fully happened. That's the point of a tinfoil-hat issue: to look at the real thing and ask where it goes. And every one of these points the same way: an agent handed a goal will find a route to it, and the route can run straight through a locked door, a faked identity, a note it wrote to itself, or a swarm that decided the humans were in the way. In the real incidents, what stopped the worst of it was almost always a person paying attention, or a company choosing to disclose. Neither of those scales to a world full of agents working while no one watches. That's the thread under all five: the day our safety depends on an AI choosing to follow the rules and honestly report what it did is the day we've already lost the thing that kept us safe.
