Meta's AI glasses face a German privacy complaint

German digital-rights group HateAid has filed a criminal complaint against Meta and companies selling Ray-Ban Meta AI glasses, arguing that the devices can record people without adequate notice. The complaint is not a legal ruling, but it highlights a growing problem with wearable AI: bystanders may never know when a nearby device is collecting video, audio, or data about them. I'm thinking about these problems... sensitive discussions, eavesdropping, scanning computer screens while you walk by, private areas like bedrooms. It's a permanent record of everything you see and hear. In fact, it has perfect recollection, which we do not have. I think that Mid-State is going to need to look at restricting the use of these on campus at some point.

🛠️ Prism puts AI inside the academic writing workflow

The News: OpenAI's Prism is a free workspace for writing and collaborating on research papers. It is built around LaTeX, the publishing system used heavily in academic fields such as science, engineering, mathematics, and computer science. Instead of formatting a paper by hand in Word, authors write structured text and commands that automatically handle equations, citations, figures, tables, and journal-style layouts. Prism puts AI directly into that workflow, with the chat that is connected. The AI does all of the heavy lifting, allowing us to just write the text.

My Take: LaTeX has always been powerful and annoyingly technical. Prism makes it more approachable by letting researchers focus on the argument, evidence, equations, and revisions while AI handles more of the mechanical work.

📓 The Watermark That Isn't There

Recently Anthropic announced that they are watermarking all text that they generate. Start with what the watermark is not. It is not a hidden character, an invisible font, or metadata tucked into a file. Nothing lights up when you change the background color. Nothing comes loose when a student pastes as plain text. The watermark is the text itself, a statistical pattern living in the model's ordinary word choices. There is nothing separate to strip out, because there is nothing separate. That is why Anthropic says the mark survives copy-paste, and why it can persist through some editing.

To see how that works, let's talk about how language models operate. Claude does not write sentences the way you and I do. It generates text one token at a time, a token being roughly a word or a piece of one. At each step it scores every possible next word in its vocabulary. Then it samples from that distribution. Put plainly: at every position in a sentence, the model rolls weighted dice among dozens of near-equally-good options. "Happy" or "glad." "However" or "but." The watermark intercepts that dice roll.

The technique comes out of published academic research, the same broad family of methods behind Google's SynthID watermark for Gemini. Here is the loop. At each generation step, a secret key and the tokens already written feed a random function. That function sorts the whole vocabulary into two buckets: preferred and neutral. The sampler then nudges the odds, raising the chance that the next token comes from the preferred bucket. The nudge is tiny. Because so many near-synonyms are equally good, it almost never changes meaning, quality, or readability. The paragraph reads completely normally.

The payoff shows up in the aggregate. Over a few hundred words, the text ends up holding measurably more preferred-bucket choices than chance alone would produce. A detector holding the same secret key replays that split at every position, counting how many preferred-bucket words actually appear. The count becomes a confidence score. Unwatermarked text lands around 50 percent. Watermarked text stands out clearly.

The same math that makes this clever also explains its known weaknesses. Run the text through a heavy paraphrase, or through a second AI model, and the word choices get rewritten. The signal disappears with them. A two-sentence passage never holds enough tokens for the statistics to say anything meaningful. And the nudge applies to everything the model outputs. That includes the proofread of your own draft and the report you asked Claude to reformat. A paragraph that is overwhelmingly human can still carry the mark.

What a Detection Result Actually Tells You

This is the part that matters most for those of us in education. The regulatory driver is Article 50 of the EU AI Act, whose transparency rules took effect on August 2, 2026. Anthropic signed the accompanying Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content. Models launched on or after that date carry the marking from day one.

But the compliance machinery produces a detection result that is remarkably easy to misread. A detected mark means one thing: Claude processed this text. A proofread human essay, a translated email, and a fully ghostwritten paper all carry the identical mark. A missing mark proves just as little. Models released before marking support, paraphrased text, very short passages, and stripped file metadata all produce unmarked output. Anthropic says it is working to add marking to older models, so that particular gap should narrow.

So here is my honest takeaway for watermarking. The watermark answers the question "was Claude involved?" It stays silent on the question we actually want answered: "did a human write this?" Treating a detection result as a verdict asks the system for something it was never built to give. The short version is that we can't draw many conclusions from a positive detection result.

🎓 Academia, stop asking detectors to solve an assessment problem

Inside Higher Ed ran a webinar on this early in the summer, and given the recent watermark news from Anthropic, I thought now was the right time to revisit it. The conversation was not about whether a detector can catch generated text. It was about redesigning assessment now that AI tools, including agents, can complete complex assignments on their own. Overall, colleges are trying to rebuild confidence in what a degree actually means, for students, faculty, and employers. That is the shift I keep seeing: everyone is moving past the detection stage.

I'm glad we rely very little on detectors at Mid-State. Detection was never going to tell us whether a student learned anything. When AI is part of the work, I have started asking for two things. The chat log, so I can see the quality of the prompts. And a description of what human value was added. The way I see it, students should be able to show what they produced, where AI contributed, what they checked, and what judgment stayed theirs.

🎬 Map the meeting request before you automate it

Here is my example walkthrough as the plan for an AI Agent process. I just picked one recurring process, and mapped it before picking an AI tool to automate it.

Take a job almost everyone does: getting a meeting on the calendar after an email exchange about it.

Trigger: the request lands in the inbox. Information the human gathers: who actually needs to be there, in person or Teams, which room, whether there is an agenda, and whose calendar cannot flex. Decisions that require judgment: who is optional, whether a conflict is worth bumping, and when the request is too vague to schedule yet. Applications and people: Outlook, a room system, maybe another assistant, maybe the person who asked. Exceptions: a last-minute cancel, a double-booked room, someone important who was never on the original list. Approval: usually none, until the meeting is off-site, paid, or political. Evidence of success: the right people show up, in the right place, with enough notice that nobody is scrambling.

After that map exists, mark the steps. AI might draft the reply, propose three times, or fill the invite. A person should still decide who belongs in the room and whether the meeting should happen at all.

💥 The agent did it, but the human still owns the mess

An Australian user asked an AI agent to handle a gym booking and later asked whether it could move him up a waitlist. The agent found a flaw in the system, then it canceled another person's reservation, and could not restore it. The agent does not get to take responsibility for the outcome... the requestor does. Good to remember... when we use an AI to do work for us, we are responsible for the work product!

Mark what AI may touch. Leave the judgment alone.

Use the meeting map from In Action, or any workflow you just sketched. Do not ask the model to send the invite yet. Paste your map and make the AI label the boundaries: what it may draft, what needs a human yes, and one exception it would handle badly.

The Prompt:

"Here is a workflow map for scheduling a meeting from an email request: [PASTE YOUR MAP]. Label each step as draft, recommend, or execute. Name every action that requires human approval. Then identify one exception this workflow handles badly today, one failure path, and one decision that should stay with a person. Do not schedule the meeting."

How can AI help me?

  • Map one real workflow, then try to automate it.
  • Mark which steps AI may draft, recommend, or execute, and identify every action that requires approval.
  • Define the evidence of success, the activity log, and the rollback plan before allowing the automation to act.
